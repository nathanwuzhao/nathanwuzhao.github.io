Challenge

More than 50 million US adults have arthritis, and a child-resistant pill bottle can mean waiting for help. Assistive arms cost thousands of dollars. TEND does the twist with under $200 of hobby parts. The bottom fingers hold the bottle while the top two slide in opposite directions, rolling the cap like palms rolling a pencil. Hobby servos have no position or force feedback, so grip force has to be inferred from motor current, and nothing may ever stall a servo against its stops.

In simulation, the full routine removes and lifts the cap in about 50 s.

System

The INA219s sit in series with the two squeeze servos' supply. The Nano is soldered to the FlexiTac reader board, and only its ICSP header is reachable, so the tactile link is a one-way SoftwareSerial TX on PB3 into a hardware UART on the ESP32.

Control

FirmwareParses S commands, writes PCA9685 pulse widths, reads both INA219s, reassembles tactile frames, and streams one D line every 40 ms.
Skills, 25 HzClosed-loop motions on the laptop: grip (current-regulated), twist, release, recenter, lift. Each has a "can it run now" check, so a skill that would screw the cap back on is refused.
SupervisorGemini picks the next skill from fixed choices, with a reason, using a camera frame plus sensor readings. The tactile map goes in as a ~150-token text grid instead of an image.
Digital twinthree.js view of the CAD that moves with live servo angles, with current plots, tactile heatmap, and a trace of every Gemini call.

Grip force comes from current. A position servo's torque grows with the gap between commanded and actual angle, so once the fingers touch, each extra degree commanded is more squeeze, and holding current rises with it. Every move starts with a 300–730 mA spike, so raw current is useless:

  1. Contact: step 3° at a time. After each step, wait for the spike to settle, read two samples, and keep the lower one. Contact is holding current minus the free-air baseline above a threshold.
  2. Regulate: nudge 1° at a time until the median holding current sits in a target band. Above a maximum, back off 2° at once.
  3. Hold during the twist: the squeeze servo is stationary while the top fingers slide, so its reading is spike-free. It's rechecked every 4 ticks and nudged ±1° to stay on target. A sharp current drop means the cap slipped.

Approaching the last known contact angle quickly and releasing only 15° cut a twist cycle from 12.9 s to 6.5 s.

Protocol

ASCII lines over USB. Telemetry, 25 Hz, 12 fields:

D <ok> <a0>…<a6> <i0_mA> <i1_mA> <tactile: 1024 hex chars>
S <idx> <deg> …Set any number of servos in one line. If any pair is invalid, nothing is applied and the reply is ERR.
C <idx> <min> <max>Per-servo pulse limits in µs, saved to flash.
R <idx> <0|1>Reverse flag for mirrored servos, saved to flash. Changing it makes the servo go limp, so it can't jump.

Tactile frames from the Nano: A5 5A + 512 bytes + 16-bit checksum, 516 bytes at ~18 frames/s, parsed byte by byte on the ESP32. ok drops to 0 if no valid frame arrived in 200 ms.

Safety

Limp at bootEvery channel stays off (angle −1) until the host sends S. A fixed boot pose once drove a servo with no end stops into a stall.
Soft limitsA calibration tool marks each servo's end of travel. Both ends sit 30 µs (~0.4 mm) inside the physical stops, so no angle from 0 to 180 can reach a stop.
Stall guardSqueeze servos back off above the current limit, and calibration goes limp above 600 mA.
AI boundaryGemini never sends servo angles. A call takes 1–3 s, but contact has to be caught within ~100 ms, so force control stays local at 25 Hz.
Port openThe host opens the ESP32-C6's USB port with DTR/RTS off. A default open resets the chip and drops whatever the gripper holds.
